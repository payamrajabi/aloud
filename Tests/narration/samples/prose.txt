The café on the corner opened in the spring, and by summer it had become the place where half the street started its day. It is not a fancy room. There are six tables, a long wooden counter and a window that catches the morning light just as the first customers arrive.

The owner, Marta, roasts her own beans in a small machine at the back. On roasting days the whole block smells faintly of toast and caramel. She will tell you, if you ask, that the secret is not the machine but the attention. She tastes every batch, writes notes in a battered notebook and changes one small thing each week.

Regulars have their habits. A retired teacher reads the paper at the same table every morning and leaves at exactly nine. Two nurses come in after their night shift and share a pastry without saying much. A boy from the bakery next door trades bread for coffee, an arrangement nobody remembers agreeing to.

What makes the place work is hard to name. The coffee is good, but there is good coffee everywhere now. Perhaps it is that nobody is in a hurry, or that Marta remembers your name after the second visit. Whatever it is, people keep coming back, and on Saturday mornings the line now reaches the door.
